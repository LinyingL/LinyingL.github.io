> _Society, which is in a state of rapid progress, constantly gives birth to new needs, and each of these is a fresh source of power for the central government, because it alone is in a position to satisfy them._
> >
> — Alexis de Tocqueville, _The Old Regime and the French Revolution_ (1856)

When Beijing celebrates the rising "discretionary share" of central transfers to its provinces — 41.7 percent in the 2026 budget, up from 31.8 percent in 2019 — it presents the trend as fiscal devolution. State media echoes the figure. Provincial governors quote it back. And the two major fiscal reforms of the past decade really did do what officials say they did. The 2014 consolidation of special-purpose grants, and the 2019 separation of "shared fiscal responsibility" payments from the main transfer pool, increased the proportion of central money that local governments deploy with fewer strings attached.

Behind that figure sits a second number, drawn from the same Ministry of Finance ledgers, that no official cites at the podium. In 2013, Chinese provinces funded 57 percent of their general public expenditure from their own tax and non-tax revenues. By 2024, that figure had fallen to 49 percent. By the 2026 budget, it stands at 49.2 percent. Provinces have lost eight percentage points of fiscal self-sufficiency in eleven years. Both numbers come from the same ledgers. Both are real.

The conventional explanation for the second trend, when anyone discusses it, is COVID. The pandemic forced provinces to spend heavily on testing, lockdowns, and stimulus while their tax bases collapsed; once the emergency ended, local revenue would recover. The argument has the merit of plausibility. It does not survive the data. The decline began long before 2020. From 2013 to 2019, six years of normal economic growth, the ratio fell from 57.0 percent to 49.6 percent. That is nearly the entire eleven-year decline, accumulated before COVID arrived. The pandemic did not start the trend. It accelerated an existing one.

A second resolution blames the property collapse. As land sale revenues fell after 2021, provinces lost a major income source and had to lean on central transfers. This explanation has more force than the first, and it is also partial. Property's contribution runs through three channels at once. Land sale revenues themselves sit in the government-managed funds budget, not the general public budget tracked here. But four taxes that _do_ sit in the general public budget are 100 percent local and tied to real estate: the deed tax, the land value-added tax, the property tax, and the urban land use tax. Together they peaked in 2021 at 1.97 trillion yuan, 17.8 percent of provincial own-source revenue. By 2024, they had fallen to 1.72 trillion, and to 14.4 percent. That single channel dragged the post-2021 trend lower by roughly three percentage points of self-sufficiency. Property explains part of the recent acceleration. It does not explain why provinces lost ground from 2013 through 2020, when these same property taxes were still rising. The answer is somewhere else.

What changed during the 2010s was the tax structure itself. In 2014, China began folding the business tax, long the largest tax controlled by local governments, into the centrally administered value-added tax. The transition completed in 2016. The reform's stated purpose was technocratic and largely correct: business tax produced cascading taxation that distorted services-sector incentives, and a unified VAT was the modern alternative. To compensate provinces for the loss of business tax revenue, the central government raised the local share of the new combined VAT pool from 25 to 50 percent. On paper, this was an even trade. In practice, two things followed that were not part of the brief. Provinces no longer set the rates, defined the base, or controlled enforcement of their largest tax source. And the reform itself was a net cut, designed to lower aggregate tax pressure, so the new 50-50 pool grew more slowly than business tax had grown before it. By 2018, the state and local tax administrations were merged into a single agency under central direction. The aim was to end the practice, common in the divided era, of provinces using local tax bureaus to lure investment with arbitrary rebates and exemptions, a step that genuine market reform required. Each of these moves had a defensible purpose. Their cumulative structural effect was to remove three of the four levers a local government in 2013 could pull to raise its own revenue.

Here is the piece most first-look explanations miss. If the reforms had transferred revenue from provinces to Beijing, you would expect the central government's share of total tax collection to have risen. It has not. In 2013, provinces collected 53 percent of total general public budget revenue. In 2024, they collected 54 percent. Beijing has not taken a larger slice of the pie. The pie itself has shrunk against the size of the economy. Total general public budget revenue fell from 21.8 percent of GDP in 2013 to 16.1 percent in 2024 — a 5.7 percentage-point drop, split almost evenly between the central level (down 2.5 points) and the provincial level (down 2.9 points). Both Beijing's revenue effort and the provinces' have weakened, in roughly the same proportion. What changed is not the split. It is the size of what is being split.

This rules out the simplest version of how the dependency arose — the inference that Beijing took provincial revenue. The provinces are more dependent on Beijing now than they were a decade ago, but not because Beijing pulled a larger share of the tax base toward the center. The real mechanism is harder to name, and harder to undo because of that.

The two trends turn out to describe two kinds of fiscal control, layered on top of each other. The first is microeconomic. It involves Beijing telling a province which projects to fund, which lines to spend on, which items to leave alone. Before 2014, this kind of control was extensive. Special-purpose grants, earmarked for specific projects and programs, accounted for over 30 percent of central transfers. Each came with conditions. A province receiving funds for a rural road would find that the same money could not be redirected to a school, even if the school needed it more.

The 2014 reform consolidated this jungle. Hundreds of grants were folded into broader categories. The 2019 reform took the largest category of conditional transfers, those for shared programs like basic medical insurance and pension subsidies, and gave them their own line, separate from genuinely flexible general transfers. By 2024, the share of central transfer payments that came with project-level conditions had fallen below 9 percent. The remaining 91 percent flowed to provinces in broader pools. Provincial finance directors today have more room to maneuver within the funds they receive than they did a decade ago. The official "fiscal autonomy" narrative captures this layer accurately.

The second kind of fiscal control is macroeconomic, and it does not move in the same direction. It concerns not how a province spends its money but how much money the province has, and where it comes from. The shrinking aggregate tax base, the loss of provincial control over rate-setting, and the erosion of property-related revenue all narrowed the share of provincial spending that provinces could finance themselves. The shortfall is plugged by central transfers. A province in 2024 has more discretion over the 41 percent of its budget that arrives from the center. A much larger share of its budget arrives from the center to begin with.

This pattern has a name in the public-finance literature. Vertical fiscal imbalance: a sustained mismatch between where revenue is raised and where it is spent. China's is now among the largest in the world. Provinces account for about 85 percent of general public spending but raise only about 53 percent of general public revenue. The gap was not designed. Looking back, the gap behaves as if it had been planned. There are two ways to close it: cede tax base back to the provinces, which Beijing has shown no inclination to do, or shrink provincial spending mandates, which would carry immediate political costs. Neither is happening. What persists, year on year, is a structure in which the _forms_ of provincial fiscal authority survive: provincial finance bureaus, provincial budget hearings, provincial People's Congresses voting on their own spending. The substance has been migrating to Beijing for over a decade.

For most of those years, a hidden subsidy obscured how far the migration had gone. Provinces ran a parallel set of accounts in the government-managed funds budget, dominated by land sale revenue, and quietly transferred large amounts from those accounts into the general public budget through a line called "transferred-in funds" (调入资金). In 2018, this transfer was 1.2 trillion yuan. By 2019, it had reached 1.9 trillion, close to 10 percent of total local spending. Provinces were not drawing down past savings. They were using the proceeds of land auctions to top up their general budgets. The mechanism worked because the land cycle worked.

When the property cycle turned, the mechanism stopped working. Land sale revenue peaked in 2021 at 8.5 trillion yuan. By 2024, it had fallen to 4.8 trillion, a 44 percent collapse over three years. Cross-budget transfers into provincial general budgets did not collapse in step, but they did fall sharply: the transferred-in funds line dropped from 1.9 trillion in 2019 to 920 billion in 2021. It has bounced between 1 and 1.7 trillion since, but the source of the rebound is no longer land. It is, increasingly, transfers from the central government's own debt issuance, the only remaining pump capable of moving that volume of money. The collapse of the land-finance subsidy did not loosen provincial dependence on Beijing. It deepened it.

Three things make this structural reading hard to argue with. The first is timing. The slope of declining self-sufficiency is steady from 2013 forward, with two visible accelerations. A one-percentage-point drop between 2015 and 2017, the years that bracket the full rollout of VAT-for-business-tax. A three-percentage-point drop between 2019 and 2022, the years in which the tax administration merger, the VAT credit refund, and the start of the property collapse all landed. Each acceleration aligns with a structural change, not a cyclical shock.

The second is geographic variation. The provinces that lost the most self-sufficiency over the past decade are not the poorest. They are the mid-tier industrial provinces — Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Hubei, Hunan — which had relied most heavily on the business tax and on locally administered enterprise taxes. Tibet, Qinghai, and the western autonomous regions, already heavily transfer-dependent before the reforms, saw their dependency change less. The reforms hit hardest where there was something to hit. That is the signature of a tax-structure change, not a macroeconomic shock that would have affected all provinces roughly equally.

The third is what the central government's own budget has had to absorb. In 2013, Beijing transferred about 80 percent of its general public budget revenue to provinces. By 2024, it transferred essentially all of it — 99.9 percent. Beijing has become, in effect, a pass-through entity, collecting revenue from the same shrinking pie that provinces collect from, and routing nearly all of it back outward, increasingly financed by central debt. The dependency is not unilateral. The center is dependent, too, on its capacity to keep redistributing.

The Ministry of Finance publishes all of this data in detail, every year. None of it is hidden. The trend rarely makes headlines because of how it is framed, not because it is concealed. Official summaries emphasize the discretion-over-spending number. The dependency figures appear in the same documents, but never in a sentence labeled as a finding. Put the tables side by side and one story emerges. China's provinces have less fiscal autonomy than they did in 2013. They also have more spending discretion. Both facts come from the same ledgers. They are two sides of the same operation.

Why, then, does this architecture exist? Almost no one designed it as such. The 2014 VAT reform was sold as tax modernization, and largely was. The 2018 tax administration merger had a real rationale in market unification and the suppression of provincial protectionism. The 2019 separation of shared fiscal responsibility payments was an accounting clarification. The shift toward broader transfer categories was framed as decentralizing decision rights. Each piece, taken alone, has defensible technocratic logic. The architecture that emerged is the cumulative effect of many separate, locally rational moves — none of which had as its explicit purpose the binding of provinces to Beijing, and all of which had that as a side effect.

What the architecture nonetheless buys, intended or not, is leverage. When Beijing decides it wants provinces to spend more on, say, basic medical insurance, it does not need to issue an order. It expands the shared-fiscal-responsibility line. Compliance follows because the alternative is a budget hole. When it wants to slow infrastructure spending, it grows the relevant grant categories more slowly than provincial demand. The center pulls a fiscal lever; provinces re-allocate on their own. This is faster than directives, harder to push back against, and easier to deflect when blame circulates. If a province cannot fund a hospital expansion, the political question "why didn't Beijing approve it?" is replaced by the technocratic question "why didn't the province manage its budget?"

The architecture's hidden cost is that it stays durable only as long as central transfers keep growing. From 2013 to 2023, transfers grew at an average of roughly 8 percent a year, well above provincial own-revenue growth. That gap is what closed the dependency loop. Provinces accepted their reduced fiscal authority because the center kept delivering more in absolute terms, and a growing land-finance subsidy filled in the rest. Both pillars are now wobbling. The 2024 budget grew transfers by 3 percent. The 2026 budget grew them by 2 percent. Land sale revenue is at half its 2021 peak. Provincial debt has continued to climb. Reports of delayed public-sector wages, once a northeastern problem, have started showing up in mid-tier provinces too.

The next decade will test what happens when the architecture itself comes under fiscal stress. If central revenue growth stalls, Beijing has three options. It can cut transfer payments, which means provinces cut services and the dependency loop tightens. It can cede tax base back to provinces, which means reversing twelve years of consolidation and rebuilding provincial autonomy. It can extend central debt to keep the system running, which is mostly what 2024 and 2025 have done. None of these options is politically free.

The third option is the only one available to the system.  Once a central authority is the sole entity capable of meeting a province's needs — when basic medical insurance, pensions and public-sector wages all depend on transfers from the capital — the authority cannot retreat without causing the very failure it was intended to prevent. Cutting transfers would result in visible service breakdowns in provinces that no longer have the tax base to fill the resulting void. Ceding the tax base back would require provincial administrations, tax bureaus and political institutions that have been hollowed out over twelve years to be reinstated. There is no operational pathway back; only a long period of reconstruction lies ahead. Each retreat would create the legitimacy crisis that the centre built its architecture to prevent.

Debt is the option that preserves the architecture. It is also the option that the architecture forces. Each year of debt-financed transfers postpones the choice between the other two options, and the system's logic — making the periphery dependent on the centre and the centre indispensable to the periphery — makes this postponement seem rational in the short term, even if it is not in the long term. This is the Tocquevillian trap: the architecture that worked by binding the provinces to Beijing has equally bound Beijing to the provinces. The centre cannot reduce its dependency without dismantling the system that gives it leverage. Dependency tightens, debt grows and, on its own terms, the architecture keeps working — until the day when debt itself becomes the binding constraint and the choice between the other two options can no longer be postponed.

**A note on data and methods**

All figures come from the Ministry of Finance Budget Department ([yss.mof.gov.cn](http://yss.mof.gov.cn)) and Treasury Department ([gks.mof.gov.cn](http://gks.mof.gov.cn)). National-level final accounts are drawn from each year's _Central-to-Local Transfer Payment Final Account Tables_ (2013–2024), _Local General Public Budget Revenue Final Account Tables_ (2013–2024), and _Local Government-managed Funds Revenue Final Account Tables_ (2013–2024). Figures for 2025 are taken from the Ministry of Finance's [January 2026 fiscal status release](http://gks.mof.gov.cn/tongjishuju/202601/t20260130_3982923.htm), which approximates final accounts pending the formal July 2026 publication. Figures for 2026 are taken from the central government's [2026 budget tables](http://yss.mof.gov.cn/2026zyczys/202603/t20260324_3986014.htm), published in March 2026 with the State Council's annual budget report.

Self-sufficiency is defined as local own-source general public budget revenue divided by local total general public budget expenditure. Dependency is defined as central transfer payments (including tax rebates) divided by the same denominator. The discretionary share within transfers excludes tax rebates and shared-fiscal-responsibility payments and is reportable only from 2019 onward, when the latter category was first separated in published accounts.

The four 100-percent-local property and land taxes used in the property-channel calculation are 契税 (deed tax), 土地增值税 (land value-added tax), 房产税 (property tax), and 城镇土地使用税 (urban land use tax), each extracted from the annual _Local General Public Budget Revenue Final Account Tables_ line items. Land sale revenue (国有土地使用权出让金收入) is taken from the _Local Government-managed Funds Revenue Final Account Tables_. Central-level revenue and central-to-local transfer payment ratios are computed from the corresponding central-government tables.
